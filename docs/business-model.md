# **BOND Business Model - TLDR**

**How we make money (3 streams):**

1. Schools and Kitas pay an annual contract (parents pay nothing) - B2B
2. Commission when parents book discounted activities (pottery, sports, etc.) -B2B
3. Margin from buying rewards in bulk, cheaper than retail - B2B

**Annual pricing:**

- Kita, public: 3,200 EUR/year (~160/child) | private: 3,800 EUR/year (~190/child)
- Grundschule, public: 3,500 EUR/year (~145/student) | private: 4,200 EUR/year (~175/student)
- Private pays more, since it isn't limited by public funding and decides faster

**Public funding channels:**

- Kitas → KiQuTG (Bavaria already has its own digitalization strategy)
- Schools → GaFöG and Schulfördervereine/Elternbeirat

**Rewards funding:** ~35% of the license fee is reserved upfront for rewards, based on modeling a Kita where 30% of families are "super-active" (reach the top reward level), 30% moderate, 40% low/occasional.

**Points system:**

- 3 pts: casual activity (30 min minimum)
- 6 pts: dedicated activity
- 15 pts: marketplace (paid) activity
- +5 pts: community challenge bonus
- Points reset quarterly

**Reward levels:** 50 / 100 / 150 / 250 pts, top level (LEGO or class) capped at 3 redemptions per family per year

**The 5 rewards:** cinema tickets, free BOND marketplace activity, Disney+/Netflix month, supermarket voucher, LEGO or music class

# BOND Business Model - Normal Version :D

## 1. Model Type

BOND runs a **B2B2C model with a B2G channel**. Institutions pay, families use BOND for free.

- **B2B2C**: BOND sells to schools, kindergartens, and Träger. Parents and children receive the app at no cost.
- **B2G**: For public schools and Kitas, BOND is sold through government-backed funding frameworks, making it a budget-approved purchase rather than an out-of-pocket cost for the institution.
- **Marketplace layer**: BOND also earns commission by connecting families to real-world activities at negotiated group rates.

This gives BOND three revenue streams instead of one: licensing, marketplace commission, and rewards margin.

---

## 2. Revenue Stream 1: Institutional Licensing

**Who pays**: Schools and kindergartens, public and private.

### Public Kitas: KiQuTG as the primary angle

KiQuTG (KiTa-Qualitäts- und -Teilhabeverbesserungsgesetz) gives each Bundesland its own budget and its own choice of focus areas ("Handlungsfelder"). Since 2025, parent engagement is no longer a mandatory federal category, so BOND cannot claim KiQuTG funds Elternarbeit everywhere. What can be said with confidence: **Bavaria** already runs an active Handlungsfeld for "Digitalisierungsstrategie in der Kindertagesbetreuung," including kita.digital.coaches supporting digital tools in Kitas in 2025 and 2026. This is BOND's strongest, most concrete funding story, and it happens to be BOND's home market. The pitch framing: KiQuTG lets each Land invest in digitalizing childcare quality, and Bavaria is already doing it.

### Public schools: GaFöG and Schulfördervereine, not Digitalpakt

Digitalpakt 2.0 (2026 to 2030, 5 billion EUR) is focused on infrastructure, hardware, and teacher training, so it is a weak fit for a parent-facing app. Two stronger, independent channels for schools:

- **GaFöG (Ganztagsförderungsgesetz)**: funds extended-day programs. BOND positions itself as part of the extended-day family wellbeing offering, not as "technology" competing for hardware budget.
- **Schulfördervereine / Elternbeirat**: the school or its parent association can buy BOND directly, bypassing government funding entirely. Useful as a fast path and as a fallback while public-sector relationships mature.

A broader, Land-independent lever worth exploring in parallel: **Krankenkassen §20 SGB V**, which funds "Lebensweltbezogene Prävention" in schools and Kitas alike, completely separate from any digital education budget.

**Private schools and Kitas**: Sold directly to the Verein or private Träger, no government intermediary needed. Private institutions are priced higher than public ones, since they are not constrained by limited public funding pools, decide faster, and typically have more flexible budgets of their own.

### Pricing structure: annual, per institution

Annual contracts, not monthly or per-semester. This matches how schools and Kitas actually budget (by school year), and it is far easier to say in a pitch than a per-child monthly calculation.

**Updated annual packages** (revised after modeling real rewards costs, see Section 4):

| Institution | Annual price | Per child / student |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kita, public (~20 children) | 3,200 EUR | ~160 EUR |
| Kita, private (~20 children) | 3,800 EUR | ~190 EUR |
| Grundschule, public (~24 students) | 3,500 EUR | ~145 EUR |
| Grundschule, private (~24 students) | 4,200 EUR | ~175 EUR |

Kita pricing sits above Grundschule because Kita content goes deeper (early childhood parenting, emotional regulation) and KiQuTG in Bavaria gives Kitas a stronger, more specific funding narrative. Private institutions carry roughly a 18 to 20 percent premium over public ones.

These prices are modeled, not yet market-tested. They should be validated with 2 to 3 pilot institutions before being locked into the deck.

Parents always use BOND for free. The institution is the paying customer, the family is the end user.

## 3. Revenue Stream 2: Experience Marketplace (Commission Model)

BOND acts as an aggregator for family activities, similar to how Wellpass or Urban Sports Club aggregate fitness studios.

**How it works**:

1. BOND negotiates bulk group rates with local activity providers (pottery studios, sports clubs, museums, workshops).
2. Example: a pottery class normally costs 50 EUR per person. BOND negotiates a volume rate of 35 EUR.
3. Families book and pay through the app at a discounted price, for example 40 EUR (still cheaper than the public 50 EUR rate).
4. The provider receives their agreed rate (35 EUR), BOND keeps the margin (5 EUR).

**Why providers say yes**: BOND brings volume and fills off-peak slots in exchange for a lower per-person rate.

**Why parents say yes**: Better price than booking directly, plus it is inside the same app they already use for BOND activities.

This stream is paid directly by parents at the point of booking. It needs no subsidy from BOND and can scale city by city as more providers are onboarded.

## 4. Revenue Stream 3: Rewards Program

This is the loop that keeps parents engaged. It works like an airline miles or credit card points program: every point needs a real monetary value behind it, and the economics only work if a fixed share of revenue is reserved in advance, matched against real usage data, not spent reactively.

### 4.1 Where the money for activities comes from, by tier

- **Free activities** (baking, playing outside, and similar): no real cost to BOND. These are DIY family activities suggested inside the app.
- **Low-cost and paid marketplace activities** (pottery, sports): paid directly by the parent at a discounted price. BOND keeps its commission spread. No subsidy needed, covered in Section 3.
- **Points-based rewards** (cinema, streaming, groceries): this is the tier that needs a funded pool, since BOND is buying real goods in bulk and giving them away against points.

### 4.2 Activity points system (separate from reward levels)

Not all activities are worth the same. Free, casual activities are effortless and happen constantly, so they earn few points. Marketplace activities already generate real commission for BOND, so they can earn more points without being a giveaway.

| Activity type | Examples | Points | Condition |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Casual / free | Walking in the park, baking cookies, stargazing, casual football | 3 pts | Minimum 30 minutes (selected via a duration dropdown when uploading proof) |
| Dedicated / intentional | Board game night, cooking project together, bike outing | 6 pts | None |
| Marketplace (paid) | Pottery, sports class, museum | 15 pts | None |
| Community challenge bonus | Any featured monthly activity | +5 pts extra | Stacks on top of the base activity points |

The 30-minute gate on casual activities exists specifically to prevent farming free points from activities that take two minutes and no real effort.

### 4.3 Reward levels and points reset

Points reset every quarter. Nothing carries over, which keeps the reward budget predictable per period instead of accumulating indefinitely.

| Level | Points | Reward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 50 pts | Free BOND marketplace activity credit |
| 2 | 100 pts | Cinema tickets |
| 3 | 150 pts | Choice of: supermarket voucher or Disney+/Netflix month |
| 4 | 250 pts | LEGO set or music/ceramics class |

**Level 4 is capped at 3 redemptions per family per year.** This keeps the top reward meaningfully aspirational without letting the most active families redeem it every single quarter, which is what originally broke the budget math (see 4.4).

### 4.4 Budget modeling: the 30 percent super-active scenario

Not every family behaves the same way. Modeling a Kita of 20 children under a working assumption of family engagement levels (to be validated against real pilot data):

| Segment | Share of families | # families (of 20) | Level reached | Real cost per family, per year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Super-active | 30% | 6 | Level 4 (cumulative, capped at 3x/year) | ~162 EUR |
| Moderate | 30% | 6 | Level 2 (cinema) | ~24 EUR |
| Low / occasional | 40% | 8 | Level 1 or below | ~4 EUR |

**Total modeled reward cost: ~1,150 EUR per year for a 20-child Kita.**

This number already includes two corrections applied after the first draft of this model:

- The 3x/year cap on Level 4 redemptions (down from an uncapped scenario of ~220 EUR per super-active family per year).
- An assumed 15 percent improvement in bulk-buy margin on the higher-tier rewards, achieved through better volume negotiation.

**Why this matters for pricing**: the reward budget needs to be roughly 35 percent of the annual license fee, not the 20 percent originally assumed, once real usage patterns are modeled instead of guessed. This is the direct reason the Kita package moved from 2,000 EUR to 3,200 EUR (public) in Section 2. The remaining revenue splits roughly 40 percent operations, 25 percent margin.

This is loyalty-program economics: the model only works if the actual share of super-active families stays near 30 percent. If a pilot shows a higher share reaching Level 4, either the reserved percentage needs to increase further, or the point thresholds need to be recalibrated upward.

## 5. How the Three Streams Work Together

| Stream | Who pays | What BOND earns | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Institutional licensing | Schools, Kitas, government funding | Annual per-institution fee | Core recurring revenue |
| Experience marketplace | Parents (discounted price) | Commission per booking | Drives real-world engagement, secondary revenue |
| Rewards program | Funded by a reserved share of licensing revenue (~35%), stretched via bulk-buy margin | Perceived value beyond cash spent | Retention and motivation loop |

The licensing revenue is the financial foundation. The marketplace and rewards program are what make the product feel valuable and sticky to parents, even though parents never pay directly.

## 6. The 5 Rewards

Chosen to be simple, easy to explain in one slide, and to reflect the business model directly rather than feel like an arbitrary prize list. A mix across parent, child, and the product's own marketplace:

| Reward | For | Why it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cinema tickets (2, family pair) | Parent and child together | Classic bulk-buy, easiest to explain |
| Free BOND marketplace activity credit (1 session, e.g. pottery or sports) | Parent and child together | Reinforces BOND's own product loop, already negotiated in Section 3, no extra procurement needed |
| Disney+ or Netflix, 1-month voucher | Parent | Simple bulk-buy, appeals to the household |
| Supermarket voucher (REWE, 20 to 30 EUR) | Parent | Practical, eases daily mental load |
| LEGO set or single music/ceramics class | Child | Directly motivates the child to want the shared activity |

Babysitting was considered but dropped from this initial list. Munich market rates run 15.50 to 20 EUR per hour (highest in Germany per Babysits data), and babysitting is a personal service, not inventory, so it cannot be bulk-negotiated the way tickets or vouchers can. Sitly and Babysits are both active and well used in Germany (Sitly alone has over 7 million users across 14 countries), but their business model is subscription-based (parents pay a premium subscription to message sitters), not per-booking. A future B2B deal would mean bulk-buying premium subscriptions for BOND parents, worth exploring as a phase 2 partnership, not part of the initial 5 rewards.

## 7. Go-to-Market by Channel

- **Public Kitas**: KiQuTG, with Bavaria's digitalization Handlungsfeld as the concrete, provable entry point.
- **Public schools**: GaFöG (extended-day funding) and Schulfördervereine / Elternbeirat as a direct, government-independent path. Krankenkassen §20 SGB V as a parallel lever for both schools and Kitas.
- **Private schools and Kitas**: Direct sales to the Verein or Träger, priced higher than public institutions, using the TUM Kita network as an early pilot and reference channel.
- **Parent to parent**: In-app invites for other families from the same class or school, encouraged through group challenges and friendly competition.
- **Word of mouth through children**: Kids talking about the activities they did drives organic interest from other families.
